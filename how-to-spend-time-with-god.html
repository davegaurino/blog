How to Spend Time with God

A relationship with God cannot grow only through occasional church attendance, quick prayers during emergencies, or reading a verse when there is extra time. Like any meaningful relationship, it needs regular attention. Spending time with God does not have to be complicated or long every day, but it should be intentional enough to give space for prayer, Scripture, reflection, and quiet listening. The purpose is not to complete a religious routine. It is to know God more personally and allow that relationship to shape the rest of your day.

Time with God is a relationship, not a religious task

Many people know they should pray and read the Bible, yet devotional time can slowly become another item on a checklist. The goal is not to complete a spiritual routine so we can feel responsible. The goal is to know God. Jesus described eternal life in relational terms: knowing the Father and the One He sent (John 17:3). Relationships grow through attention, honesty, listening, trust, and time. Spending time with God should include discipline, but discipline serves relationship. If you miss a day, do not turn guilt into another reason to stay away. Return. God is not waiting to shame you for an imperfect routine. He invites you to come near (James 4:8).

Choose a regular time and protect it

Consistency becomes easier when devotional time has a place in the day. Jesus often sought quiet time with His Father, including early in the morning (Mark 1:35). Daniel had regular times of prayer (Daniel 6:10). Morning works well for many people because it lets Scripture shape the mind before messages, news, and responsibilities rush in. Others may have a quieter evening schedule. The exact hour is less important than making the time real. Start with a manageable period such as twenty or thirty minutes. Put the phone away. Let people in your household know that this is quiet time when possible. A protected appointment becomes more consistent than a vague intention to pray “sometime today.”

Begin with a simple prayer for openness

Before reading, ask God to teach you. Psalm 119:18 asks God to open the eyes so wonderful things can be seen in His law. Prayer does not need impressive language. Speak naturally and respectfully. Tell God where your mind is. If you are distracted, say so. If you are angry, confused, thankful, afraid, or excited, bring that into the conversation. The Psalms show that honest prayer can include praise, questions, confession, grief, and trust. God already knows what is in the heart. Prayer is not informing Him of facts; it is opening the heart to Him.

Read Scripture slowly enough to hear it

It is possible to read several chapters and remember almost nothing. Quantity can be useful, especially when reading through the Bible, but devotional reading should also include reflection. Choose a passage and ask: What does this show me about God? What does it show about human character? Is there a command, warning, promise, example, or principle? How does this connect with the larger biblical story? What should change in my thinking or behavior? Second Timothy 3:16-17 describes Scripture as useful for teaching, correction, and preparation for good work. The Bible is meant to shape life, not simply increase information.

Pray from what you have read

One way to keep prayer from becoming repetitive is to let Scripture guide it. If you read about forgiveness, confess resentment and ask for strength to forgive. If you read about God’s care, thank Him for specific ways you have been provided for. If you read a promise, ask for faith to trust it. If you read a warning, examine your life honestly. Jesus’ model prayer includes worship, submission to God’s will, daily needs, forgiveness, and protection from temptation (Matthew 6:9-13). You can use those themes as a framework while still speaking in your own words.

Include quiet, not only words

We are surrounded by noise. Even when no one is speaking, a phone can provide endless sound and stimulation. Psalm 46:10 calls us to be still and know that God is God. Quiet does not mean trying to empty the mind into nothing. It can mean sitting with a passage, thinking about God’s character, remembering His faithfulness, or listening for conviction through truths already revealed in Scripture. Do not expect a mysterious voice. God’s Word is the standard. Quiet simply creates space for thoughts to settle and for us to stop controlling the conversation with constant words.

Keep a prayer list, but do not let prayer become only requests

Lists can help us remember people, responsibilities, decisions, and needs. Pray for family, friends, leaders, church, work, people who are suffering, and those who do not know God. First Timothy 2:1 encourages a broad life of prayer. Yet if prayer consists only of requests, our relationship with God can become transactional. Include praise, gratitude, confession, surrender, and listening through Scripture. Keep a record of answered prayers if that encourages you. Sometimes an answer is yes, sometimes no, sometimes wait, and sometimes God changes the person praying more than the circumstance.

Walk with God during ordinary activities

Devotional time is important, but God is not present only during a morning routine. First Thessalonians 5:17 calls believers to pray continually. We can speak briefly with God while commuting, cooking, walking, waiting, or beginning a difficult task. Thank Him when something good happens. Ask for patience before a conversation. Confess quickly when you speak badly. Pray for a person as soon as you hear of a need. This ongoing awareness turns faith from a separate compartment into part of daily life. Formal prayer and spontaneous prayer strengthen each other.

Let the Sabbath deepen your time with God

The seventh-day Sabbath creates extended space for relationship with God. At creation God blessed and sanctified the seventh day (Genesis 2:2-3), and the fourth commandment calls us to remember it (Exodus 20:8-11). The day is not simply about avoiding work. It is a gift of time. Attend worship. Study Scripture without rushing. Walk in nature and remember the Creator. Eat with family or friends. Visit someone who is lonely. Sing. Pray. Rest. Isaiah 58:13-14 describes the Sabbath as a delight. A weekly day with fewer ordinary demands can restore a devotional life that has become hurried.

Do not measure closeness to God only by emotion

Some devotional times feel warm and meaningful. Others feel dry. Faith should not be built entirely on emotional intensity. A marriage is not real only on days when conversation feels exciting; commitment also appears in ordinary faithfulness. The same principle applies spiritually. Psalm 42 shows a believer longing for God while feeling discouraged. Keep praying when feelings are weak. Keep reading when a passage seems ordinary. Keep worshiping when life is confusing. Over time, habits of faith create deep roots. Emotion is a gift, but Scripture and God’s character are more stable than emotion.

Make time with God the center that orders the rest of life

When we spend regular time with God, priorities become clearer. Scripture corrects our ambitions. Prayer exposes anxiety. Worship reminds us that we are not the center of the universe. Confession keeps pride from hardening. Sabbath teaches us to stop. Hope in Christ’s return keeps temporary problems and achievements in perspective (John 14:1-3). Start simply. Choose a time tomorrow. Read a passage. Pray honestly. Sit quietly for a few minutes. Repeat. Do not wait until you can create the perfect devotional routine. Relationships grow through regular presence. God invites you to know Him, and time is one of the clearest ways we answer that invitation.

Worship and study with other believers

Personal devotion is essential, but the Christian life was never designed to be entirely private. Hebrews 10:24-25 encourages believers to gather, encourage one another, and stimulate love and good works. Corporate worship exposes us to Scripture, prayer, singing, testimony, service, and perspectives beyond our own. A small Bible study group can also help because other people notice details we miss. Ask questions. Compare passages. Pray for one another. The seventh-day Sabbath provides a natural weekly rhythm for this shared worship. Personal time with God and community worship should strengthen each other rather than compete.

Keep showing up through dry seasons

There may be periods when prayer feels ordinary and Bible reading does not produce strong emotion. Do not assume God is absent. Farmers do not dig up seed every day to see whether it is growing. Psalm 1 compares the person rooted in God’s instruction to a tree planted by water. Roots develop through consistency. During a dry season, simplify rather than quit. Read one Psalm. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly. Walk outside and thank God for what you see. Read one Gospel story and imagine the scene carefully. Keep the relationship open. Feelings often return, but faithfulness does not need to wait for them.

Let time with God lead to obedience

Devotion is incomplete if it never changes behavior. Jesus connected love for Him with keeping His commandments (John 14:15). If prayer reveals that you need to apologize, do it. If study exposes dishonesty, correct it. If God’s Word calls you toward generosity, give. If Scripture shows the need for rest, honor the Sabbath. If it reveals a harmful habit, begin changing it. Obedience turns devotional insight into lived faith. Over time, this is one of the clearest signs that time with God is becoming more than a routine.

Choose a realistic time and place where you can meet with God regularly, even if you begin with only a few quiet minutes each day. Bring your Bible, speak to God honestly, listen through His Word, and carry one thought from that time into the rest of your day. Start small if necessary, but start consistently and allow the relationship to deepen over time.
